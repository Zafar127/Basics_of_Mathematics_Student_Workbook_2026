Matrix Size and Entries

Foundations · dimensions and notation

Problem

Given the matrices below, state their sizes, find \(a_{12}, a_{23}, b_{21}, b_{32}\), and write the second row of \(A\) and the first column of \(B\) as vectors.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&3\\0&4&5\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-2&3\\4&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

A matrix's size is its number of rows by its number of columns. The entry \(a_{ij}\) is in row \(i\), column \(j\).

Solution

  • \(A\) has 2 rows and 3 columns: size \(2\times3\).
  • \(B\) has 3 rows and 2 columns: size \(3\times2\).
  • Reading the indicated positions gives \(a_{12}=-1,\ a_{23}=5,\ b_{21}=-2,\ b_{32}=1\).

The second row of \(A\) is \((0,4,5)\), and the first column of \(B\) is

\[\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\4\end{pmatrix}.\]
Check: The row has three entries, matching the columns of \(A\); the column has three entries, matching the rows of \(B\).
02

Addition and Scalar Multiplication

Foundations · entrywise operations

Problem

For the matrices \(A\) and \(B\), compute \(A+B,\ A-B,\ 3A-2B\). Explain why addition requires matrices of the same size.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\-1&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\0&5\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

Addition and subtraction operate entry by entry. Scalar multiplication multiplies each entry by the scalar. Addition is possible only when entries in corresponding positions exist in both matrices.

Solution

\[A+B=\begin{pmatrix}1+4&2-2\\-1+0&3+5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\-1&8\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[A-B=\begin{pmatrix}1-4&2+2\\-1-0&3-5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-3&4\\-1&-2\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[3A=\begin{pmatrix}3&6\\-3&9\end{pmatrix},\quad 2B=\begin{pmatrix}8&-4\\0&10\end{pmatrix},\quad 3A-2B=\begin{pmatrix}-5&10\\-3&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]
Check: Each answer is \(2\times2\). For example, the top-right entry of \(3A-2B\) is \(3(2)-2(-2)=10\).

When Can Matrices Be Multiplied?

Foundations · dimension compatibility

Problem

Given \(A_{2\times3}, B_{3\times4}, C_{4\times2}, D_{2\times2}\), decide whether \(AB, BA, BC, CB, AC, CA, AD,\) and \(DA\) are defined. Give each defined product's size.

Concept

A product \((m\times n)(p\times q)\) is defined exactly when the inner dimensions agree, \(n=p\). The result has the outer dimensions \(m\times q\).

Solution

ProductDimension checkDefined?Result
\(AB\)\((2\times3)(3\times4)\)Yes\(2\times4\)
\(BA\)\((3\times4)(2\times3)\), \(4\ne2\)No—
\(BC\)\((3\times4)(4\times2)\)Yes\(3\times2\)
\(CB\)\((4\times2)(3\times4)\), \(2\ne3\)No—
\(AC\)\((2\times3)(4\times2)\), \(3\ne4\)No—
\(CA\)\((4\times2)(2\times3)\)Yes\(4\times3\)
\(AD\)\((2\times3)(2\times2)\), \(3\ne2\)No—
\(DA\)\((2\times2)(2\times3)\)Yes\(2\times3\)
Check: Every defined product has matching inner dimensions; its outer dimensions determine the result size.
04

Matrix Multiplication and Noncommutativity

Foundations · order matters

Problem

Compute \(AB\) and \(BA\), then explain what the comparison shows.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\3&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

Each product entry is the dot product of a row of the first matrix and a column of the second. Matrix multiplication is not generally commutative.

Solution

\[AB=\begin{pmatrix}1(2)+2(3)&1(0)+2(1)\\0(2)+1(3)&0(0)+1(1)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}8&2\\3&1\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[BA=\begin{pmatrix}2(1)+0(0)&2(2)+0(1)\\3(1)+1(0)&3(2)+1(1)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\3&7\end{pmatrix}.\]

Since \(AB\ne BA\), reversing the order changes the result in this example. This is the meaning of noncommutativity.

Check: Both products are \(2\times2\), but even their top-left entries differ: 8 versus 2.
05

Matrix Times Vector

Foundations · column combinations

Problem

Compute \(Ax\) and express the result as a linear combination of the columns of \(A\).

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad x=\begin{pmatrix}4\\2\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

Multiplying a matrix by a vector can be computed by row dot products, or viewed as a linear combination of the matrix columns weighted by the vector entries.

Solution

\[Ax=\begin{pmatrix}2(4)+(-1)(2)\\1(4)+3(2)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}6\\10\end{pmatrix}.\]

With columns \(c_1=(2,1)^T\) and \(c_2=(-1,3)^T\), the vector entries give the coefficients:

\[Ax=4c_1+2c_2=4\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}+2\begin{pmatrix}-1\\3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}6\\10\end{pmatrix}.\]
Check: Both methods agree, and a \(2\times2\) matrix times a \(2\times1\) vector gives a \(2\times1\) vector.
06

Transpose

Foundations · reversing rows and columns

Problem

Find \(A^T,\ B^T,\ AB\), then verify that \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\).

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

Transposition exchanges rows and columns. Transposing a product reverses the order of its factors.

Solution

\[A^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\2&5\\3&6\end{pmatrix},\qquad B^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[AB=\begin{pmatrix}1+4-3&0+2+9\\4+10-6&0+5+18\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&11\\8&23\end{pmatrix},\quad (AB)^T=\begin{pmatrix}2&8\\11&23\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[B^TA^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\2&5\\3&6\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&8\\11&23\end{pmatrix}=(AB)^T.\]
Check: Direct multiplication gives the same matrix on both sides of the identity.

Identity, Zero, and Matrix Powers

Foundations · neutral elements and patterns

Problem

For \(A\), find \(AI,\ IA,\ A+0,\ A^2,\ A^3\). Explain the roles of the identity and zero matrices, and describe the power pattern.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

The identity matrix is the multiplicative neutral element; the zero matrix is the additive neutral element. A matrix power is a repeated product.

Solution

With \(I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(0\) the \(2\times2\) zero matrix,

\[AI=IA=A,\qquad A+0=A.\]
\[A^2=\begin{pmatrix}4&4\\0&4\end{pmatrix},\qquad A^3=A^2A=\begin{pmatrix}8&12\\0&8\end{pmatrix}.\]

The diagonal entries follow \(2^n\); the upper-right entries are \(n2^{n-1}\). This suggests, for \(n\ge1\),

\[A^n=\begin{pmatrix}2^n&n2^{n-1}\\0&2^n\end{pmatrix}.\]
Check: The upper-right entry in \(A^3=A^2A\) is \(4(1)+4(2)=12\).

Row Operations and Their Reversibility

Foundations · controlled transformations

Problem

Starting with \(A\), perform in order \(R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1\), \(R_3\leftarrow R_3+R_1\), and then swap \(R_2\) and \(R_3\). Give the inverse operation for each step.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\2&4&1\\-1&1&3\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

A row replacement \(R_i\leftarrow R_i+kR_j\) is reversed by using \(-k\); a row swap is reversed by repeating the same swap.

Solution

  1. \(R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1\): the new row is \((2,4,1)-2(1,2,-1)=(0,0,3)\).
    \[\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&0&3\\-1&1&3\end{pmatrix}\]
    Reverse with \(R_2\leftarrow R_2+2R_1\).
  2. \(R_3\leftarrow R_3+R_1\): the new row is \((-1,1,3)+(1,2,-1)=(0,3,2)\).
    \[\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&0&3\\0&3&2\end{pmatrix}\]
    Reverse with \(R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_1\).
  3. Swap \(R_2\) and \(R_3\):
    \[\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1\\0&3&2\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}\]
    Reverse by swapping \(R_2\) and \(R_3\) again.
Check: Adding \(2R_1\) back to the first changed second row restores \((2,4,1)\).
09

Composing Transformations

Foundations · order of transformations

Problem

For \(x=(1,2)^T\), compute \(RSx\) and \(SRx\). Compare their geometric meanings.

\[S=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

The rightmost transformation acts first. \(S\) doubles the horizontal coordinate; \(R\) rotates \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise.

Solution

Scale first, then rotate:

\[Sx=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad RSx=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\2\end{pmatrix}.\]

Rotate first, then scale:

\[Rx=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad SRx=\begin{pmatrix}-4\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The results differ. Scaling first doubles the original horizontal component, which becomes vertical after rotation. Scaling afterward doubles the already-rotated horizontal component instead.

Check: \((-2,2)^T\ne(-4,1)^T\), so the order matters.

Columns of a Matrix Product

Foundations · the product column rule

Problem

Find \(Ab_1,\ Ab_2,\ AB\), where \(b_1,b_2\) are the columns of \(B\).

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\-1&0\\3&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

If \(B=[b_1\ b_2]\), then \(AB=[Ab_1\ Ab_2]\). Entry \(i\) of \(Ab_j\) is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\)—exactly entry \((i,j)\) of \(AB\). Thus each column of \(AB\) equals \(Ab_j\).

Solution

\[b_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix},\quad b_2=\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad Ab_1=\begin{pmatrix}1-2+0\\0-1+3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\end{pmatrix},\quad Ab_2=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]
\[AB=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}.\]
Check: The columns of the product are \((-1,2)^T=Ab_1\) and \((2,1)^T=Ab_2\), as the entry-by-entry definition predicts.

When Do Matrices Commute?

Challenge · symbolic matrix equations

Problem

Find the conditions on \(a,b,c,d\) such that \(AB=BA\), and describe all matrices \(B\) with this property.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

Two matrices are equal exactly when all corresponding entries are equal, so compare the products entry by entry.

Solution

\[AB=\begin{pmatrix}a+c&b+d\\c&d\end{pmatrix},\qquad BA=\begin{pmatrix}a&a+b\\c&c+d\end{pmatrix}.\]

Equating corresponding entries gives \(c=0\) and \(d=a\); \(a\) and \(b\) are unrestricted. Hence

\[B=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&a\end{pmatrix}.\]
Check: Substitution gives \(AB=BA=\begin{pmatrix}a&a+b\\0&a\end{pmatrix}\).

Formula for a Matrix Power

Challenge · pattern and induction

Problem

Compute \(A^2,A^3,A^4\), conjecture a formula for \(A^n\), and prove it by induction.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

Induction proves a statement for every positive integer by checking a base case and showing that the case \(n\) implies \(n+1\).

Solution

\[A^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad A^3=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad A^4=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The pattern suggests \(A^n=\begin{pmatrix}1&n\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). For \(n=1\), this is \(A\), so the base case holds. Assume the formula holds for some \(n\ge1\). Then

\[A^{n+1}=A^nA=\begin{pmatrix}1&n\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&n+1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

This is the same formula with \(n+1\), completing the induction.

Check: Substituting \(n=2,3,4\) recovers the computed powers.

A Recurrence Written in Matrix Form

Challenge · Fibonacci sequence

Problem

Compute \(F^2\) through \(F^5\), relate the entries to the Fibonacci sequence, and explain the action of \(F\) on \((a,b)^T\).

\[F=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

The Fibonacci sequence satisfies \(F_{k+1}=F_k+F_{k-1}\), starting from \(F_0=0,\ F_1=1\). A matrix can update a pair of consecutive terms.

Solution

\[F^2=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad F^3=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix},\quad F^4=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\3&2\end{pmatrix},\quad F^5=\begin{pmatrix}8&5\\5&3\end{pmatrix}.\]

These entries follow consecutive Fibonacci numbers; in general, \(F^n=\begin{pmatrix}F_{n+1}&F_n\\F_n&F_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}\) for \(n\ge1\). Also,

\[F\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a+b\\a\end{pmatrix}.\]

When \(a,b\) are consecutive terms, this produces their sum as the next term and retains \(a\) for the next step.

Check: \(F^5\) contains \(8,5,5,3\), in agreement with the sequence.

Rotation Matrices

Challenge · angle addition

Problem

Multiply \(R(\alpha)R(\beta)\) and show using the angle-sum identities that it equals \(R(\alpha+\beta)\).

\[R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

Use \(\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\) and \(\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta\).

Solution

\[R(\alpha)R(\beta)=\begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta & -\cos\alpha\sin\beta-\sin\alpha\cos\beta\\ \sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta & -\sin\alpha\sin\beta+\cos\alpha\cos\beta \end{pmatrix}.\]

Applying the angle-sum identities gives

\[R(\alpha)R(\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha+\beta)&-\sin(\alpha+\beta)\\\sin(\alpha+\beta)&\cos(\alpha+\beta)\end{pmatrix}=R(\alpha+\beta).\]

Geometrically, rotating by \(\beta\) and then by \(\alpha\) combines into a rotation by \(\alpha+\beta\).

Check: All four entries match the defining pattern of \(R(\alpha+\beta)\).

Associativity and Calculation Paths

Challenge · grouping and operation counts

Problem

Compute \((AB)C\) and \(A(BC)\), compare the results, and explain associativity and the operation counts.

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Concept

Matrix multiplication is associative: compatible products have the same result regardless of grouping. For dense multiplication of an \(m\times n\) matrix by an \(n\times p\) matrix, the standard count is \(mnp\) scalar multiplications and \(mp(n-1)\) scalar additions.

Solution

First grouping:

\[AB=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\1&4\end{pmatrix},\qquad (AB)C=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\1&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}10&3\\2&-3\end{pmatrix}.\]

Second grouping:

\[BC=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\4&1\\-2&-4\end{pmatrix},\qquad A(BC)=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\4&1\\-2&-4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}10&3\\2&-3\end{pmatrix}.\]

For \((AB)C\), forming \(AB\) uses \(2\cdot3\cdot2=12\) multiplications and \(2\cdot2\cdot(3-1)=8\) additions. Multiplying the \(2\times2\) result by \(C\) uses 8 multiplications and 4 additions. Total: 20 multiplications and 12 additions.

For \(A(BC)\), forming \(BC\) uses \(3\cdot2\cdot2=12\) multiplications and \(3\cdot2\cdot(2-1)=6\) additions. Multiplying \(A\) by the \(3\times2\) result uses 12 multiplications and 8 additions. Total: 24 multiplications and 14 additions.

The intermediate dimensions differ, so the calculation costs differ even though associativity gives the same final matrix.

Check: Both paths produce \(\begin{pmatrix}10&3\\2&-3\end{pmatrix}\), a \(2\times2\) matrix.
